# Script — "NCMHCE Exam Format Explained (2026)"

Target length: 8–10 minutes. Talking-head or voiceover + on-screen graphics.
Facts current for exams taken through June 30, 2027 (the 2027 change is covered at the end).

---

**[0:00 — COLD OPEN, no intro]**

The NCMHCE is eleven case studies. That's the whole exam. No standalone trivia
questions, no vocabulary section — eleven clients, and about a hundred scored
decisions about what you'd do with them. In the next few minutes I'll show you
exactly how a case is built, what's actually scored, the one thing about the
format that fails well-prepared people — and how the exam changes in 2027.

**[0:30 — WHAT YOU'LL FACE]**

On exam day you get eleven case studies. One of them is unscored — it's being
field-tested — but you won't know which, so you treat all eleven as real.

Each case starts with a narrative: an intake, and then the case moves forward
in time through sessions. Here's the part most people get wrong before they
start studying: **the diagnosis is given to you.** This is not a
guess-the-diagnosis exam anymore. The question is never "what does this client
have" — it's "you're this client's counselor; what do you do."

Each case carries roughly nine to fifteen multiple-choice questions, about one
hundred scored across the exam. Every question has four options and exactly
one best answer.

**[1:45 — THE THREE SECTIONS]**

The questions inside a case always move through the same three phases, in
order — and this order is the real structure of the exam:

First, **assessment and conceptualization**. What do you assess NEXT? What's
the best formulation? You're building your clinical picture.

Second, **treatment planning**. Given what you found, what's the best next
step? Which intervention fits this client, now?

Third, **counseling skills and ethics**. The case throws you a session moment —
a disclosure, a boundary push, a safety concern — and asks for your best
response, or what ethics requires.

Notice what that sequence is: it's just how real clinical work flows. Intake,
plan, respond. If you've sat with clients, you already know the shape — the
exam tests whether you can hold the line under pressure.

**[3:00 — HOW IT'S SCORED, AND THE TRAP]**

Options aren't all equally wrong. The best answer earns full credit; the
distractors are graded clinical errors — a slightly-off choice, a premature
choice, and a genuinely harmful one. That's why "I narrowed it to two" happens
constantly on this exam: the second-best option is designed to be defensible.

The tie-breaker is almost always **sequence**. When a question says FIRST or
NEXT, it is testing order, not knowledge. There's a whole priority ladder
behind those questions — a fixed order of what outranks what in a session —
and it's learnable. I'll show you one rung of it in a second; the full ladder
is what we train inside PassReady Prep, because more passing candidates are
separated from failing ones by FIRST/NEXT discipline than by any diagnosis
fact.

**[4:30 — WALK ONE WITH ME]**

[On screen: a sample vignette — use one retired case from the PassReady bank.]

Quick demo. The intake says the client was given a diagnosis of panic disorder;
she's had two ER visits with clean cardiac workups, and she's started avoiding
driving. Question: "What do you assess NEXT?" Four options: begin interoceptive
exposure, assess the scope of her avoidance, refer back to cardiology, explore
childhood attachment. Exposure is where treatment is headed — but it's an
intervention, and we're still in assessment. Cardiology is already done —
that's the premature-reassurance trap. Attachment isn't what this moment needs.
Assess the avoidance: that's NEXT. One decision, in order. That's the exam,
about a hundred times.

**[6:00 — LOGISTICS, FAST]**

You'll sit the exam at a Pearson VUE center or via remote proctor. Plan for a
long morning — the session runs around four hours, and the case format is a
reading-stamina exam as much as a clinical one. Check your NBCC handbook for
your exact time allowance and your state's registration path. Scores are
scaled; you'll get a pass/fail with section feedback if you don't pass — and if
that happens, the section feedback tells you exactly which of the three phases
to drill.

**[7:00 — THE 2027 CHANGE]**

One more thing, because you'll see confusing info out there: NBCC is updating
the exam on **July 1, 2027** — ten case studies instead of eleven, built on a
new content outline. If you're testing before then, everything in this video is
your format. If you're testing after, the case-study structure stays the
same — the skills in this video transfer — but check NBCC's 2027 exam
specifications for the updated outline.
