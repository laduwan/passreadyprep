# Script — "How I'd Study for the NCMHCE in 2026"

Target length: 9–11 minutes. Companion to the format video — reference it, don't repeat it.

---

**[0:00 — COLD OPEN]**

If I had eight weeks to pass the NCMHCE, I wouldn't start with a textbook, and
I wouldn't start with flashcards. I'd start by failing a practice case on day
one — on purpose. Here's the whole plan: what to do each week, what to skip,
and the one habit that moves your score more than anything else.

**[0:30 — THE PRINCIPLE]**

The NCMHCE is eleven case studies where the diagnosis is handed to you and
every question is a decision: what do you assess NEXT, what's the plan, how do
you respond. (If that's news, watch my format video first — it's linked below
and in the card above.) So the skill being tested isn't recall. It's
**clinical sequencing under pressure**. You build that the same way you built
every clinical skill: reps with feedback. Content review supports the reps —
it never replaces them.

**[1:30 — WEEKS 1–2: DIAGNOSE YOURSELF]**

Week one, take a full-length mock exam cold. You will hate your score. Good —
it's a baseline, not a verdict, and it does two things: builds reading stamina
for a four-hour exam, and tells you precisely where you lose points. When you
review it, sort every miss into one of three buckets: didn't know the content,
knew it but broke sequence — answered an intervention when the case was still
in assessment — or misread the vignette. Most people discover the middle bucket
is the biggest. That's actually great news, because sequencing is the fastest
thing to fix.

Week two, drill your two weakest content areas with individual cases — not
passive reading. Work a case, miss, read the rationale, log the rule you broke.

**[3:30 — WEEKS 3–6: THE CASE-A-DAY ENGINE]**

This is the core of the plan: **one full case every day, reviewed properly.**
A case takes twenty to thirty minutes; the review takes just as long, and the
review is where the score moves. For every question you missed — and every one
you got right but hesitated on — say out loud why the keyed answer wins NOW,
in this phase of the case. If you can't say it in one sentence, that's a
content gap; go read that one thing. Nothing builds the exam's rhythm —
assess, plan, respond — like daily immersion in it.

Twice a week, add a focused content block — thirty minutes, one topic, always
chosen by your misses, never by the table of contents. The high-yield short
list, because the exam leans on them: suicide and homicide risk assessment,
duty to warn, mandated reporting, intake and crisis sequencing, and the
treatment-of-choice pairings for the big diagnostic families. Notice those are
mostly decision topics, not memorization topics.

And keep a **rules notebook**. Every miss becomes one line — one sequencing
rule you broke, in your own words. For example: "assessment before
intervention." By week six it's twenty-some lines, and together they start to
form a priority ladder — a fixed order of what outranks what on this exam. You
can build yours rung by rung from your own misses, or learn the full ladder
the fast way inside PassReady Prep, where every case rationale names the rung
it's testing.

**[6:30 — WEEKS 7–8: SIMULATE AND TAPER]**

Week seven: two full mocks under strict exam conditions — timed, one sitting,
no snacks-and-phone breaks that the real exam won't give you. You're training
decision fatigue now; case eight is where unpracticed candidates start
misreading vignettes. Between mocks, review with the same three buckets from
week one and watch the middle bucket shrink.

Week eight: taper. Daily singles in your weak areas, reread the rules
notebook, and stop learning new content three days out — new material this
late adds anxiety, not points. Last 48 hours: sleep, logistics, one light
case to stay warm. Walk in with your rules, not with flashcards.

**[8:00 — THREE MISTAKES THAT FAIL PREPARED PEOPLE]**

One: studying like it's the NCE — memorizing theories and vocabulary for an
exam that tests decisions. Two: unlimited content review as procrastination —
if most of your study time isn't working cases, you're preparing for the wrong
exam. Three: reviewing only your wrong answers — the right-but-hesitant ones
are where exam-day time pressure will hurt you.
