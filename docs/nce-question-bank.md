### 185. nce-s-cor-001 · Counseling and Helping Relationships · empathy and its levels · medium · In review

A 34-year-old client says, "My boss praised my project in front of everyone, but then gave the promotion to someone else. I don't know what to think." Which counselor response is an interchangeable (Level 3) response on Carkhuff's empathy scale?

**A. [ ]** You should ask your boss directly why you were passed over this time.
> Advice-giving ignores the client's feelings entirely; it is subtractive (Level 1 or 2), not interchangeable.

**B. [✔]** You feel confused because the praise and the promotion decision don't match.
> This captures the feeling (confusion) and its stated cause at the same depth the client expressed, which is the definition of a Level 3 interchangeable response.

**C. [ ]** Underneath the confusion, you may fear that your hard work goes unrecognized.
> This goes beyond what the client expressed to an underlying feeling and meaning, making it additive (Level 4), not interchangeable.

**D. [ ]** Lots of people get passed over at work, and it usually works out fine in the end.
> Reassurance minimizes the experience and shifts away from the client's feelings, a subtractive response.

**Rationale:** Carkhuff's scale rates counselor responses from 1 to 5. Level 3 is the minimally facilitative, interchangeable level: the counselor reflects essentially the same feeling and meaning the client expressed, adding nothing and taking nothing away. Levels 1 and 2 subtract from the client's message, and Levels 4 and 5 add depth beyond it.

**References:** Gladding (Counseling: A Comprehensive Profession) — Carkhuff's scale of empathic understanding, Levels 1-5

### 186. nce-s-cor-002 · Counseling and Helping Relationships · empathy and its levels · hard · In review

In a sixth session, a graduate student says, "I keep telling everyone I'm fine with moving home after graduation. My parents are happy, so I guess I should be too." Which counselor response BEST illustrates additive (Level 4) empathy?

**A. [ ]** So you're moving home after graduation, and your parents are pleased with it.
> This paraphrases the content but omits feeling; it is at best Level 2, slightly subtractive.

**B. [ ]** You feel fine about moving home, since your parents are happy with the plan.
> This repeats the client's surface statement and misses the doubt in "I guess I should be"; it does not add depth.

**C. [ ]** Why do you think your parents' happiness matters so much in this decision?
> A why-question invites intellectualizing and does not communicate understanding of the feeling, so it is not an empathic response.

**D. [✔]** You sound unsure, as if your own wishes got set aside to keep your parents happy.
> This names the implicit ambivalence and the meaning beneath it (setting aside one's own wishes), adding accurately to what the client said: Level 4.

**Rationale:** Additive empathy (Carkhuff Levels 4-5) goes beyond the client's explicit words to reflect feelings and meanings the client implied but did not fully state, which helps the client explore at a deeper level. To be additive rather than inaccurate, the reflection must stay grounded in cues the client gave, such as the hedged "I guess I should be."

**References:** Gladding (Counseling: A Comprehensive Profession) — Advanced (additive) empathy, Carkhuff Levels 4-5

### 188. nce-s-cor-004 · Counseling and Helping Relationships · empathy and its levels · easy · In review

A client tearfully describes the recent death of her dog. Which counselor response reflects empathy rather than sympathy?

**A. [ ]** I'm so sorry; I lost my own dog last year and I still cry about it sometimes too.
> This shifts the focus to the counselor's own loss and expresses pity or shared distress, which is sympathy and self-disclosure, not empathy.

**B. [ ]** That is such a sad thing to go through, and I feel terrible for you today.
> Feeling sorry for the client is sympathy; it conveys the counselor's feeling rather than understanding of the client's.

**C. [✔]** Losing her has left a real emptiness in your days, and the grief feels heavy.

### 189. nce-s-cor-005 · Counseling and Helping Relationships · unconditional positive regard · medium · In review

A 52-year-old client says, "I yelled at my teenage son last night and called him useless. I'm ashamed to even tell you." Which counselor response BEST demonstrates unconditional positive regard?

**A. [ ]** That was harsh, but I'm sure you are a good parent most of the time anyway.
> Reassurance about being a "good parent" is conditional praise; it evaluates the client rather than accepting the client.

**B. [ ]** Calling him useless can do real damage, so let's plan what to say instead.
> This jumps to evaluation and correction before the client feels accepted; it is not an expression of UPR.

**C. [✔]** It took courage to share that, and I'm glad you trusted me with it here.
> This accepts the client as a person, without approving or condemning the behavior, and values the client's openness, which is UPR.

**D. [ ]** Don't be ashamed, since all parents lose their temper and say things they regret.
> Telling the client not to feel shame dismisses the feeling and normalizes too quickly; it is reassurance, not acceptance.

**Rationale:** Rogers described unconditional positive regard as a warm acceptance of the client as a person, without conditions of worth. UPR does not mean approving of every behavior; it means the client's worth is not contingent on what is disclosed, which lowers defensiveness and supports self-exploration.

**References:** Corey (Theory & Practice) — Person-centered therapy: unconditional positive regard and acceptance

### 190. nce-s-cor-006 · Counseling and Helping Relationships · congruence and genuineness · medium · In review

Midway through a session, a client asks, "You look distracted. Are you even listening to me?" The counselor realizes her attention had drifted. Which response BEST demonstrates congruence?

**A. [✔]** You're right, my mind wandered for a moment. I'm with you now; please go on.
> The counselor's words match her inner experience, she owns it briefly, and she returns the focus to the client, which is congruence.

**B. [ ]** Of course I'm listening, and I have been following everything you've said.
> Denying a lapse the counselor knows occurred is incongruent; outward words do not match inner experience.

**C. [ ]** It sounds like you worry people don't listen to you. Tell me more about that.
> Reflecting the client's concern here deflects from the counselor's actual lapse and avoids genuineness.

**D. [ ]** I've had a long, stressful day with other clients, so I'm tired and distracted.
> This is honest but over-discloses and shifts the focus to the counselor's needs rather than the relationship.

**Rationale:** Congruence (genuineness) means the counselor's outward behavior is consistent with inner experience, without a professional facade. Rogers viewed it as the most basic of the core conditions. Being genuine does not require disclosing every feeling; the counselor shares what is relevant to the relationship and keeps the focus on the client.

**References:** Corey (Theory & Practice) — Person-centered therapy: congruence or genuineness
