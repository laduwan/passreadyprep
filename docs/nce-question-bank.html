A supervisor tells a trainee, "Every feeling you have toward a client, not just the conflicts from your own past, can be useful information about the client and the relationship." This statement reflects which view of countertransference?
A. Classical view, treating it as an obstacle to be eliminated
The classical (Freudian) view limits countertransference to the counselor's unresolved conflicts and treats it as interference.
B. Behavioral view, treating it as a learned response to extinguish
Countertransference is not framed as a conditioned response in behavioral theory; this distractor misattributes the concept.
C. Complementary view, limited to the client's projected roles
The complementary conception frames countertransference as the counselor's reaction to the interpersonal pull of the client's style, a narrower idea than all feelings toward the client.
D. Totalistic view, including all counselor reactions to clients ✔ KEY
The totalistic view defines countertransference as all of the counselor's emotional reactions and sees them as potentially useful data.
Rationale: The classical view, rooted in Freud, defines countertransference narrowly as the counselor's unconscious, conflict-based reactions to the client's transference and treats it as an impediment. The totalistic view broadens it to all of the counselor's emotional responses and treats them as a source of understanding. Contemporary integrative views keep the focus on conflict-based reactions while stressing that, once understood, they can inform the work.
References: Corey (Theory & Practice) — Psychoanalytic therapy: countertransference and its therapeutic use

198. nce-s-cor-014 · Counseling and Helping Relationships · nonjudgmental stance · medium · In review

A college student says, "I've been hooking up with different people most weekends, and my roommate thinks I'm a mess." Which counselor response BEST reflects a nonjudgmental stance?
A. Your roommate may have a point, so let's look at the risks you're taking.
Siding with the roommate conveys the counselor's judgment before understanding the client's view.
B. Your roommate sees it one way. I'm curious how you feel about these weekends. ✔ KEY
This neither condemns nor endorses the behavior and invites the client's own perspective, which is nonjudgmental.
C. There's nothing wrong with that at all, and your roommate should not judge you.
Approval is also an evaluation; it imposes the counselor's values in the opposite direction.
D. What do you think is missing in your life that leads you to keep doing this?
This presumes the behavior signals a deficit, a judgment embedded in the question.
Rationale: A nonjudgmental stance means the counselor suspends both disapproval and approval and seeks to understand the client's experience from the client's frame of reference. Counselors avoid imposing their own values on clients, which allows clients to explore their choices without becoming defensive.
References: ACA Code of Ethics — A.4.b Personal Values

199. nce-s-cor-015 · Counseling and Helping Relationships · warmth and respect · easy · In review

A 70-year-old widower arrives early for his first session and seems nervous. Which counselor behavior BEST conveys warmth and respect?
A. Greet him by name, ask how he prefers to be addressed, and offer him a seat ✔ KEY
This shows genuine interest and respects his preferences and dignity, communicating warmth and respect.
B. Call him "sweetie" and speak slowly and loudly so he can follow along easily
Endearments and exaggerated speech are elderspeak; they are patronizing and disrespectful.
C. Hand him the intake forms and ask him to wait until his appointment time
This is procedural and distant, and it ignores his visible nervousness.
D. Tell him there is no need to be nervous because most clients feel fine later
This dismisses his feelings and offers reassurance in place of attunement.
Rationale: Warmth is conveyed through friendly, caring verbal and nonverbal behavior, and respect is shown by treating the client as a capable person with preferences and dignity. Asking how a client wishes to be addressed is a simple, culturally responsive way to communicate both, and avoiding ageist communication such as elderspeak is part of respect.
References: Gladding (Counseling: A Comprehensive Profession) — Building a counseling relationship: warmth and respect

200. nce-s-cor-016 · Social and Cultural Diversity · awareness of power and privilege in the counseling relationship · medium · In review

A White counselor working with a Black client hears the client say, "I wasn't sure I could talk about racism at work with you." Which counselor response BEST shows awareness of power and privilege in the relationship?
A. I don't really see color, so you can talk to me about anything you need to here.
Color-blind statements deny the relevance of race and the client's concern, which is a microinvalidation.
B. I've had many Black clients, so I understand a lot about what you face at work.
Claiming understanding based on experience with other clients overstates the counselor's knowledge and dismisses the concern.
C. As a White counselor I may miss things, and I'd like to hear it if you're willing. ✔ KEY
This names the racial difference and the limits of the counselor's perspective, and invites the client's experience without pressure.
D. Let's focus on what you can control at work, since we can't change the people there.
Redirecting away from racism minimizes a systemic experience and avoids the topic the client raised.
Rationale: Counselors from privileged groups hold both professional and social power. Broaching racial and cultural differences directly and nondefensively, and acknowledging the limits of one's own perspective, signals that the client's experiences of oppression are welcome. Color-blind or minimizing responses are common microaggressions that weaken trust.
References: Sue & Sue (Counseling the Culturally Diverse) — Power, privilege, and microaggressions in counseling; color-blindness
