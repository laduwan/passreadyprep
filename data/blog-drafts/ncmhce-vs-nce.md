If you are finishing a counseling program or starting your post-graduate hours, you have probably heard two acronyms used almost interchangeably: the NCE and the NCMHCE. They are not interchangeable. They are different exams with different formats and different goals, and the one you need depends on where you plan to be licensed and which license you are pursuing. Here is how they differ and how to find out which one applies to you.

## The short version

- The **NCE** (National Counselor Examination) is generally described as a broad knowledge exam covering the foundations of professional counseling. It has 200 multiple-choice questions, of which 160 are scored and 40 are unscored field-test items.
- The **NCMHCE** (National Clinical Mental Health Counseling Examination) is built around clinical simulation cases that ask you to make decisions about a client as the case unfolds. It has 11 case studies, each with 9 to 15 multiple-choice questions; 100 questions are scored.
- Which exam you take is set by your **state licensing board**, not by personal preference. Some boards require one, some require the other, and some accept either or require both for different license levels. Your state board's website is the place to confirm which one applies to you.
- Both exams are developed by the National Board for Certified Counselors (NBCC) and delivered through Pearson, at a test center or online through Pearson's remotely proctored OnVUE platform.

## What each exam is really testing

### The NCE: breadth of counseling knowledge

The NCE asks whether you know the knowledge base of the profession. Prep materials commonly organize it around areas such as professional practice and ethics, counseling theories, human development, group work, assessment, career development, research, and social and cultural foundations. NBCC's current content outline organizes the scored items into six domains: Counseling Skills and Interventions, Areas of Clinical Focus, Professional Practice and Ethics, Intake, Assessment, and Diagnosis, Treatment Planning, and Core Counseling Attributes. The first two carry the most items.

A typical NCE question is self-contained. It gives you a short stem and asks you to pick the best answer. Many items reward accurate recall: which theorist is associated with a concept, what a stage model describes, what an ethics code requires in a given situation.

### The NCMHCE: clinical judgment in context

The NCMHCE asks a different question: can you act like a competent clinical mental health counselor with a specific client? Instead of standalone items, you work through cases. You read intake information, and then you make decisions about assessment, diagnosis, treatment planning, intervention, and ethics as the case develops. Diagnoses and presenting problems run through the cases, and you are expected to reason about them in context.

Knowledge still matters, but it is used in service of decisions. You need to know the deciding feature between two diagnoses, which screening tool fits the situation, and which action comes first when several are reasonable. Much of the challenge is sequencing: deciding what has to happen first when several actions are appropriate.

## How the two exams feel different on test day

**Question style.** NCE items tend to be independent of each other. NCMHCE questions are linked to a case, so earlier details shape later answers.

**Kind of difficulty.** On the NCE, a hard question is often one where you don't remember the fact. On the NCMHCE, a hard question is often one where you know all four options are reasonable and have to decide which one comes first.

**Scoring.** Both exams are criterion-referenced: you are measured against a passing standard, not against other test-takers. On each, every scored question counts for one point, a panel of subject matter experts sets the passing score, and statistical equating adjusts it for each exam form. Starting July 1, 2027, the NCMHCE reports a scaled score from 100 to 500, with 360 as the passing point.

**Length and timing.** Both exams have a total test session of 255 minutes (4 hours and 15 minutes), which includes the tutorial and agreement screens. The NCMHCE gives you 225 minutes for the cases, with a scheduled 15-minute break after the fifth case.

## How to find out which exam you need

Don't rely on classmates, forum posts, or even this article for the final answer. Requirements change, and they differ by state and license tier. Instead:

1. **Go to your state licensing board's website** and find the requirements for the specific license you are pursuing (for example, an associate-level license versus a full independent license).
2. **Look for the exam requirement by name.** Confirm whether the board names the NCE, the NCMHCE, or accepts either, and for which license level.
3. **Check timing rules.** Some boards let you test before graduation or early in supervised practice, while others require certain hours first. Your board's application instructions spell this out.
4. **Plan for a move.** If you might be licensed in another state later, check that state's requirements too, since portability rules vary from state to state.
5. **Ask your program or supervisor.** Then confirm with the board directly.

## Do you ever need both?

It depends on your path. Some counselors take one exam for an initial license and the other later for a different license level, a move to another state, or a national credential. If you are unsure, ask your board directly whether a passing score on one exam will satisfy every license you expect to hold. If there is any chance you will need both, it can make sense to prepare for the NCE's broad knowledge base first and then build NCMHCE-style clinical reasoning on top of it, since the knowledge carries over.

## Preparing for each one

### If you need the NCE

Focus on breadth and recall. Work through each content area systematically, use spaced-repetition flashcards for theorists, stage models, and ethics, and take timed practice exams weighted to the content outline. Track accuracy by domain so you can spend your time where you are weakest.

### If you need the NCMHCE

Focus on decision-making. Practice deciding what comes first when several actions are reasonable, until it is automatic. Study diagnoses through their clinical presentation and their differentials, not just their criteria lists. Above all, work full cases and debrief every one, spending as much time on why the wrong options lose as on why the right one wins.

### If you need both

Start with the NCE content areas to build your foundation, then shift toward case-based practice. PassReady Prep offers [NCE practice and mock exams](/nce.html) as a separate product alongside its NCMHCE case simulations, so you can prepare for both in one place.

## Bottom line

The NCE tests what you know about counseling. The NCMHCE tests what you do with a client in front of you. Your state board decides which one you need, so confirm the requirement for your license level before you build a study plan. Once you know your exam, prepare for its style: breadth and recall for the NCE, clinical judgment and sequencing for the NCMHCE.
